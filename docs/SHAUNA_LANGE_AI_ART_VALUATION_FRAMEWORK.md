# Formalizing an AI-Native Art Valuation Model

## Executive Summary

This thought paper proposes an AI-native valuation model for physical, born-digital, hybrid, and AI-generated artworks. It is built around Shauna Lee Lange’s core insight: art valuation is moving from expert narrative into structured intelligence.

The historical art market priced works through a mix of connoisseurship, reputation, scarcity, provenance, and institutional validation. Those variables still matter. The difference is that they are now becoming measurable. Authorship can be mapped as a network graph. Provenance can be ranked by evidentiary strength. Condition can be treated as a risk-adjusted discount rate. Liquidity can be modeled as exit probability. Geography can be expressed as a market spread. Timing can be scored as a market-cycle coefficient. Digital visibility and resonance can be measured as persistence, replay, circulation, cultural uptake, and trust-weighted attention.

The central claim is simple:

> Art valuation is no longer only a matter of taste. It is becoming a structured intelligence problem.

This framework does not replace expert judgement. It organizes expert judgement into a repeatable, auditable, and AI-compatible architecture.

The output should not be a single magic number. The model should produce:

1. **Baseline price band** based on comparables and stated valuation purpose.
2. **Provenance confidence score** based on evidentiary strength.
3. **Resonance score** based on cultural signal strength and persistence.
4. **Liquidity score** based on the probability and speed of resale.
5. **Risk-adjusted valuation range** based on condition, drift, legal exposure, timing, geography, and market manipulation risk.

In Reactor Theory / RT11 language:

```text
Valuation Coherence = (Identity × Evidence × Demand × Control) / Drift
```

Where:

- **Identity** = artist, authorship, career stage, institutional position
- **Evidence** = provenance, documentation, title, authenticity
- **Demand** = liquidity, collector depth, market comparables
- **Control** = condition, legal clarity, appraiser discipline, sale strategy
- **Drift** = uncertainty, gaps, volatility, fraud risk, poor timing, weak market placement

For AI art, the model expands further:

```text
AI Artwork Value = Authorship × Provenance × Condition × Liquidity × Geography × Timing × Narrative × Resonance × Technical Novelty
                   minus Drift, Reproducibility Risk, Rights Risk, Fraud Risk, and Market Manipulation
```

The highest-value works are not merely attractive outputs. They are coherent cultural assets: identifiable, evidenced, scarce or meaningfully bounded, resonant, liquid enough for market confidence, and strong enough to survive time.

## 1. The Problem: Art Valuation Is Still Too Narrative

The traditional art market has always understood value, but it has not always expressed value in a structured way.

A painting may be described as important because of the artist, a prior owner, a gallery, an exhibition, a catalogue essay, a museum wall label, or a collector story. Those signals matter. The problem is that they are often bundled into narrative language without being ranked, weighted, or tested.

That creates several weaknesses:

- provenance can be treated as a story rather than evidence;
- condition can be described without being priced as risk;
- celebrity ownership can be over-weighted;
- thin liquidity can be ignored;
- geography can be mistaken for global value;
- timing can be treated as luck instead of a coefficient;
- social visibility can be dismissed even when it is forming demand;
- AI-generated work can be under-valued or over-valued because the market lacks a shared classification system.

Lange’s framework corrects this by making the appraisal process explicit. It asks not only, “What is the work?” but also:

- Who made it?
- How certain is authorship?
- How strong is the evidence?
- How complete is the ownership chain?
- What is the condition risk?
- How liquid is the artist’s market?
- Where should the work be sold?
- When should it be introduced?
- What is the correct valuation purpose?
- Is the market signal real or manufactured?
- Does the work have cultural resonance beyond price?

This is the shift from narrative provenance to structured provenance, and from subjective pricing to structured valuation intelligence.

## 2. Core Principle: The Artwork as a Layered Asset

An artwork is not a single object from a valuation perspective. It is a layered asset.

At minimum, every work carries the following layers:

1. **Object layer** — what the thing physically or digitally is.
2. **Authorship layer** — who made it, and how confidently that is known.
3. **Provenance layer** — where it has been, who owned it, and what evidence supports that chain.
4. **Condition layer** — what state it is in and what risks are embedded in that state.
5. **Market layer** — comparable sales, demand, liquidity, and collector depth.
6. **Context layer** — exhibitions, literature, gallery representation, institutional placement, and critical discourse.
7. **Geographic layer** — where the strongest demand exists.
8. **Timing layer** — when the market is most receptive.
9. **Narrative layer** — why the work matters now.
10. **Resonance layer** — how deeply the work propagates through culture.
11. **Governance layer** — whether rights, title, authenticity, and records are controlled.

The mistake of the old market is not that it valued the wrong things. The mistake is that it often failed to separate these layers cleanly.

A strong artist with weak provenance is not the same as a weak artist with perfect provenance. A highly resonant digital work with poor rights clarity is not the same as a fully documented but culturally inert output. A rare work with no buyer depth is not the same as a liquid work with broad collector demand.

The valuation system must preserve these differences.

## 3. The Seven-Layer Traditional Valuation Stack

### 3.1 Authorship

Authorship remains the primary price engine.

The first valuation question is still: who made it?

But authorship is no longer just a biography. It is becoming a graph.

Key parameters:

- artist identity;
- career stage;
- known body of work;
- number of known works created;
- number of known works at market;
- institutional validation;
- gallery representation;
- exhibition history;
- collector network power;
- auction history;
- digital footprint;
- social and network analytics.

Tools and data sources include Artfacts, Artnet, Artprice, MutualArt, auction house archives, gallery data, fair disclosures, Artsy, Getty Research Institute archives, and blockchain platforms such as Verisart and Codex Protocol.

The modern authorship model should map:

```text
Artist → Galleries → Collectors → Museums → Auctions → Critics → Media → Market Confidence
```

The valuation insight:

> Identity is turning into a graph rather than a biography.

For emerging artists, this matters enormously. Career stage can now be quantified through network centrality: who shows them, who collects them, which institutions validate them, and how often their work circulates through reputable channels.

Image recognition and reverse image search add another layer. They can identify whether a work has appeared before, detect duplicates, and trace circulation across galleries and fairs. This reduces fraud risk and strengthens attribution confidence.

### 3.2 Provenance

Provenance is moving from narrative storytelling into structured data.

Key parameters:

- ownership history;
- artist authentication;
- studio records;
- invoices;
- gallery labels;
- customs documents;
- exhibition records;
- institutional records;
- scholarly references;
- publications;
- legal title;
- restitution risk;
- documentary gaps;
- reputational exposure.

The governing rule:

> The closer the document is to origin, the stronger the valuation support.

This is the heart of Lange’s Provenance Hierarchy.

### Lange’s Provenance Hierarchy — 10-Step Evidentiary Scale

| Tier | Evidence Class | Valuation Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| A | Artist authentication and origin | strongest evidence; signature, certificate, studio record, foundation letter, catalogue raisonné inclusion, artist correspondence |
| B | Early market entry | first gallery invoice, bill of sale, consignment agreement, early collector record, lifetime exhibition record |
| C | Continuous ownership | auction records, dealer invoices, private sales contracts, estate documents, inheritance records, ownership chain continuity |
| D | Institutional validation | museum exhibition records, loan agreements, condition reports from conservation institutions, curated exhibition catalogues |
| E | Scholarly and market publication | catalogue raisonné, monographs, academic articles, press reviews, gallery catalogues, raisonné supplements |
| F | Logistical and movement records | shipping documents, customs forms, import/export licenses, storage receipts, transit condition reports, insurance records |
| G | Forensic and material evidence | scientific analysis, pigment testing, canvas weave analysis, X-ray or infrared imaging, frame analysis, conservation reports |
| H | Legal and database checks | Art Loss Register, Interpol database, restitution databases, cultural property registries, liens, seizure files, insurance verifications |
| I | Contextual and circumstantial evidence | photos in situ, installation views, archive images, letters implying ownership, oral histories, interviews |
| J | Weak signals and attributions | stylistic opinion, unattributed labels, unverifiable certificates, gallery stickers without records, anecdotal claims, period-style frames unsupported |

The hierarchy turns provenance into a scoring system. It does not treat every document as equal. A direct artist letter does not carry the same weight as a later anecdote. A museum loan record is not the same as a gallery sticker. A shipping receipt is useful, but it does not prove authorship on its own.

In an AI valuation system, provenance should become a structured graph:

```text
Object → Creator → First Record → Ownership Events → Exhibition Events → Publication Events → Conservation Events → Legal Checks → Current Title
```

Each node receives:

- evidence type;
- source reliability;
- date confidence;
- document integrity;
- proximity to origin;
- contradiction score;
- legal risk flag;
- reputational risk flag.

The output is not merely “provenance exists.”

The output is:

```text
Provenance Confidence = completeness × evidentiary strength × continuity × contradiction control
```

### 3.3 Condition

Condition operates as both physical reality and financial discount rate.

Key parameters:

- surface stability;
- paint adhesion;
- craquelure;
- flaking;
- blanching;
- support integrity;
- canvas tension;
- tears, punctures, and warping;
- lining history;
- restoration history;
- inpainting;
- overpainting;
- varnish removal;
- reversibility of interventions;
- pigment fading;
- binder degradation;
- varnish yellowing;
- support oxidation;
- cleanliness and surface contamination;
- frame abrasion;
- stretcher marks;
- environmental exposure;
- storage history;
- conservation documentation;
- visual coherence.

The valuation insight:

> Condition is not only descriptive. It is a discount rate.

AI imaging, spectral analysis, machine vision, tension mapping, and degradation forecasting can convert conservation language into risk-adjusted pricing. A condition report should not sit outside the model as a PDF. It should become structured data.

A practical condition score should separate:

1. **Structural stability** — will the work survive?
2. **Surface integrity** — how much original visible material remains?
3. **Restoration burden** — how much intervention has occurred?
4. **Reversibility** — can prior intervention be undone?
5. **Environmental history** — has the work lived in stable or damaging conditions?
6. **Visual coherence** — does the work still present well to the market?
7. **Documentation quality** — are condition reports professional, recent, and detailed?

For AI and digital art, condition becomes file integrity:

- original file hash;
- resolution and master-file quality;
- metadata preservation;
- version control;
- storage redundancy;
- display dependency;
- hardware/software dependency;
- smart contract stability;
- platform risk;
- link rot risk;
- archival format risk.

### 3.4 Liquidity

Liquidity determines how reliably an artwork can become cash again.

Key parameters:

- transaction frequency;
- sell-through rate;
- buyer pool depth;
- price variance;
- gallery support;
- institutional absorption;
- time-to-sale;
- comparable market activity;
- geographic buyer diversity;
- market-maker support;
- resale pathway.

The art market behaves like finance here.

A work is not only worth what someone might pay. It is worth what can be realized within a given time window, in a specific market, under specific conditions.

Liquidity scoring should include:

```text
Liquidity Score = transaction frequency + sell-through rate + buyer depth + price stability + time-to-sale efficiency + gallery support
```

A strong artist may still have weak liquidity if few works trade. Scarcity can increase value but reduce exit probability. Conversely, works on paper or mid-scale formats may command lower prices but sell more easily due to a larger buyer pool.

Rule of thumb:

- highly liquid artist: little or no discount, sometimes premium;
- moderately liquid artist: standard market band;
- thin market artist: 20–40% liquidity discount;
- trophy-only market: high headline value but fragile exit probability.

### 3.5 Geography

The same artwork can have different values in New York, London, Hong Kong, Paris, Seoul, or private advisory channels.

Key parameters:

- city-specific comparables;
- regional collector demand;
- currency environment;
- tax environment;
- auction house placement strategy;
- cross-border capital flow;
- buyer identity distribution;
- import/export constraints;
- freeport and storage infrastructure;
- regional institutional demand.

The valuation insight:

> The painting does not have one price. It has a price map.

A work sold at Christie’s New York in May may not produce the same result as the same work sold in Hong Kong in November. That spread is not noise. It is geography as coefficient.

Advanced valuation should model arbitrage:

```text
Geographic Value Creation = stronger-market sale estimate - weaker-market acquisition cost - transaction costs - timing risk
```

This is already how sophisticated advisors operate. AI will make it more visible.

### 3.6 Timing

Timing can move value materially.

Key parameters:

- market cycle;
- auction seasonality;
- interest rates;
- equity market confidence;
- global liquidity;
- collector sentiment;
- inventory pressure;
- exhibition momentum;
- media visibility;
- recent sell-through rates;
- comparable supply in the same season.

The same painting can shift 20–50% depending on when it enters the market.

Timing should be modeled through:

```text
Timing Coefficient = cycle position × seasonality × macro liquidity × sentiment × inventory pressure
```

Peak windows matter:

- New York: May and November;
- London: March and October;
- Hong Kong: March and September;
- fair-driven cycles: Basel, Miami Beach, Hong Kong, Frieze, Armory, TEFAF.

A strong work in the wrong cycle can underperform. A properly placed work during peak collector compression can outperform materially.

### 3.7 Professional Appraiser Context

Valuation purpose determines valuation output.

Before any number is assigned, the appraiser must define the use case.

The same work may have different values depending on whether the context is:

- fair market value;
- insurance replacement value;
- estate or tax valuation;
- lending collateral value;
- liquidation value;
- private sale estimate;
- auction estimate;
- institutional acquisition review;
- charitable donation valuation;
- litigation support.

Without purpose, the number is unstable.

Purpose-specific outputs:

| Purpose | Likely Valuation Behavior |
|---|---|
| Fair market value | market-normalized estimate under ordinary conditions |
| Insurance replacement | often higher due to sourcing difficulty and replacement burden |
| Liquidation | discounted for speed and constrained buyer set |
| Lending collateral | conservative, risk-adjusted, liquidity-sensitive |
| Auction estimate | strategic range designed to attract bidding |
| Private sale | relationship-driven and confidentiality-sensitive |
| Institutional acquisition | provenance, title, and significance weighted heavily |

## 4. The AI Art Valuation Extension

AI art requires a broader valuation question:

> Is the work just output, or is it culture?

Traditional art valuation begins with a stable object and an identified artist. AI art complicates that. It may involve a human prompt author, a model, a dataset, a workflow, a curator, a post-production process, a token, a platform, and a collector community.

Additional AI art parameters:

- prompt authorship;
- prompt originality;
- model provenance;
- model version;
- dataset transparency;
- human curation level;
- post-production intervention;
- workflow documentation;
- reproducibility risk;
- output uniqueness;
- technical novelty;
- aesthetic distinction;
- cultural resonance;
- collector adoption;
- institutional legitimacy;
- rights clarity;
- edition control;
- smart contract integrity;
- metadata permanence;
- storage permanence;
- platform dependence.

The AI-native valuation stack should separate seven questions:

1. **Who authored the creative direction?**
2. **What system generated or assisted the output?**
3. **How transparent is the workflow?**
4. **How unique is the result?**
5. **How much human judgement shaped the final work?**
6. **How strongly does the work resonate culturally?**
7. **Can the work survive legally, technically, and culturally?**

The model should not reward raw generation alone. It should reward governed creative signal.

## 5. Ten-Tier AI Art Ranking Model

A public-facing AI art model can classify works into ten tiers.

| Tier | Category | Description | Valuation Meaning |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 10 | Cultural Landmark | defines or reshapes an era | scarce cultural infrastructure; highest long-term importance |
| 9 | Institutional Masterpiece | museum-level importance with strong method, legitimacy, and lasting art-historical potential | institutional-grade asset |
| 8 | Collector Blue Chip | high quality, strong authorship, strong demand, and proven market performance | high-confidence collector asset |
| 7 | Emerging Master | outstanding work by emerging or rising artist with strong provenance and growing adoption | high-upside growth asset |
| 6 | Solid Investment | high-quality AI art with strong execution and growing recognition | investable with moderate risk |
| 5 | Established Quality | well-crafted work with consistent execution and a defined audience | credible mid-market asset |
| 4 | Promising Work | compelling work with partial proof and developing market signal | early-stage opportunity |
| 3 | Developing Work | early value or experimental piece with interesting ideas but limited provenance or market adoption | speculative signal |
| 2 | Speculative / Decorative | primarily decorative or novelty work with weak evidence and high uncertainty | low-confidence purchase |
| 1 | Commodity Output | low originality, weak cultural signal, easily reproducible | minimal collectible value |

The key distinction:

> AI art is not just image quality. It is culture, context, authorship, proof, and market adoption.

A beautiful image can be Tier 1 if it is generic, undocumented, and culturally inert. A rougher work can rank higher if it introduces a new visual language, carries strong authorship, has clear provenance, and catalyzes a cultural movement.

```text
C = Condition / Control
L = Liquidity
G = Geography
T = Timing
N = Narrative / Cultural Context
R = Resonance
S = Scarcity / Uniqueness
A = AI-specific authorship and workflow integrity
D = Drift / uncertainty / risk
```

Traditional valuation coherence:

```text
VC = (I × E × L × C × G × T × N) / D
```

AI-native valuation coherence:

```text
AIVC = (I × E × C × L × G × T × N × R × S × A) / D
```

Because raw multiplication can over-punish new artists and emerging categories, production systems should use a weighted geometric mean:

```text
Coherence Quality = exp(sum(weight_i × ln(score_i))) - Risk Penalties
```

```text
Coherence Score = 100 × exp(
```

## 8. Governance and Verification

The valuation model must distinguish between three separate claims:

1. **Verified** — the record or credential is valid.
2. **Credible** — the evidence meaningfully supports the claim.
3. **Valuable** — the market assigns economic value to the supported claim.

A blockchain record can be verified but not valuable. A certificate can be authentic but weak. A viral AI image can be resonant but legally unstable. A famous artist name can be powerful but attribution-contested.

The model should not collapse these categories.

### 8.1 Recommended Governance Stack

- provenance representation standards;
- signed media credentials where available;
- appraiser-defined valuation purpose;
- evidentiary hierarchy scoring;
- risk scoring and reason codes;
- human expert override;
- audit logs;
- dispute resolution;
- update cadence;
- model card publication;
- bias and manipulation testing.

### 8.2 Dispute Resolution Ladder

1. Automated challenge submitted.
2. Evidence freeze.
3. Independent provenance review.
4. Condition / legal / attribution review if needed.
5. Model re-score.
6. Human expert determination.
7. Public correction or private revision depending on context.
8. Audit record preserved.

### 8.3 Reason Codes

- authorship dispute;
- documentary contradiction;
- chain-of-title gap;
- restitution risk;
- forged certificate risk;
- condition uncertainty;
- market manipulation risk;
- wash trading risk;
- bot-amplified resonance;
- unclear AI workflow;
- rights mismatch;
- platform dependency;
- token/media separation risk.

## 10. Case Scenario: Salvator Mundi

The Salvator Mundi illustrates why valuation must be range-based rather than single-number based.

### Authorship

Attributed to Leonardo da Vinci, the painting sits at the absolute ceiling of brand power. That creates a theoretical valuation tier above nearly any modern or contemporary artist. However, attribution has been contested among scholars. In a conventional model, that uncertainty triggers a discount. In this case, the Leonardo attribution still dominates, but disagreement introduces structural volatility.

### Provenance

The provenance is partially reconstructed with gaps. The painting’s documented history runs from early modern European collections into obscurity, then re-emerges in the 20th century. Normally, the absence of a continuous chain would suppress value. Here, the rediscovery narrative amplified it.

A data-driven model would produce a mixed signal:

```text
High historical significance + incomplete continuity + extreme brand power + major narrative amplification
```

### Condition

Condition is where risk intensifies. The painting underwent extensive restoration prior to its 2017 sale. Large portions of the visible surface are conservation intervention rather than untouched original material. In a strict condition index, this produces a significant discount.

### Liquidity

Liquidity is paradoxical. At $450.3 million in 2017, the painting set a record. But liquidity at that level is extremely thin. The buyer pool is limited to sovereign wealth funds, top-tier institutions, and a small number of ultra-high-net-worth individuals.

### Geography

Geography amplifies the valuation field. New York provided global auction theatre and Western institutional validation. Middle Eastern institutional ownership provides capital concentration and prestige acquisition. The painting carries different values depending on where it is activated.

### Timing

Timing was decisive. The 2017 sale occurred during a strong global art-market cycle and was placed into a contemporary evening sale, reframing the work as a trophy asset rather than only an Old Master.

### Model Output

A 2028-style AI valuation model would likely produce extreme dispersion:

```text
Lower bound: condition + attribution uncertainty + thin liquidity
Upper bound: Leonardo brand + uniqueness + geopolitical demand + cultural visibility
```

The lesson:

> Trophy value is not normal liquidity. It is geopolitical resonance plus scarcity plus theatre.

## 11. Case Scenario: Degas Drawing at Christie’s

**Work:** Edgar Degas, *Quatre danseuses à mi-corps*  
**Sale context:** The Joanna Carson Collection: A Legacy of Glamour and Giving  
**Estimate:** USD 800,000 – USD 1,200,000

### Authorship

Degas sits in a fully institutionalized tier. His dancer works are among the most recognizable and liquid subcategories in Impressionism. Authorship strength is very high.

Artists need more than output. They need structured records.

Future-facing artists should preserve:

- creation records;
- studio documentation;
- prompt and workflow records for AI art;
- edition details;
- exhibition history;
- sale records;
- collector communications;
- provenance packets;
- digital asset hashes;
- media mentions;
- institutional interactions.

The artist who controls their archive controls future valuation confidence.

### 12.2 For Collectors

Collectors need to think like underwriters.

Before acquisition, ask:

- Is authorship clear?
- Is provenance ranked or merely described?
- Is condition documented?
- Is there buyer depth?
- Is the market liquid?
- Is the geography favorable?
- Is the timing right?
- Is the narrative real?
- Is there resonance beyond hype?
- Is the legal and title status clean?

### 12.3 For Appraisers

Appraisers will not be replaced by AI. But appraisers who use structured intelligence will outperform those who rely only on prose.

The appraiser’s future role is to:

- define valuation purpose;
- classify evidence;
- inspect risk;
- validate comparables;
- interpret anomalies;
- explain uncertainty;
- produce defensible ranges;
- govern the model output.

### 12.4 For Marketplaces

Marketplaces that standardize provenance, condition, liquidity, and resonance metrics will become more trusted than marketplaces that only display images and prices.

Future listings should include:

- provenance confidence;
- condition score;
- liquidity band;
- geography score;
- timing score;
- resonance history;
- rights clarity;
- valuation purpose;
- audit trail.

### 12.5 For Institutions

Museums and institutions should use the model conservatively.

Institutional acquisition should overweight:

- provenance;
- legal title;

- scholarship;
- long-term public relevance.
